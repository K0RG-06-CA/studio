# Model halation as linear back-reflected exposure

Halation is a secondary exposure. Light passes through the emulsion stack, crosses the transparent base, reflects off the base–air interface (or the camera's pressure plate) and re-enters the emulsion at a displaced position ([US 5246823](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5246823); [Bard & Dunn, J. SMPTE 1971](https://journal.smpte.org/periodicals/Journal%20of%20the%20SMPTE/80/7/11/)). Because the red-sensitive layer sits next to the base, the returning light exposes red first, so halos read red, turning orange where the source is bright enough to reach the green layer ([NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/node/4236); [Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/)). The optics are linear: a fixed small fraction of the transmitted light is redistributed. The apparent "threshold" comes from the log tone curve, not from the light. A physically based effect should therefore convolve **scene-linear exposure before the characteristic curve**, with no keying, using per-channel weights of R ≫ G > B ≈ 0 and kernel radii fixed in micrometres on the film plane. The open-source spektrafilm (formerly agx-emulsion) project already implements this design. It is GPL-3.0, so its structure and parameters can be ported directly into a GPL-3.0 Rust editor ([spektrafilm GitHub](https://github.com/andreavolpato/spektrafilm)). The weak point is calibration. **No source found publishes a measured halation PSF, halo radius or energy fraction for any modern stock**, so every current number is either derived from geometry or tuned by eye, and the editor will need its own bracketed point-source scans to validate.

## Total internal reflection sets a ring near 1.8× base thickness

The classical mechanism comes from Mees. Light that strikes the back surface squarely reflects straight back and does not spread the image. The lateral spread comes from light that silver-halide grains scatter obliquely into the support. Rays at small angles partly escape through the back, which leaves a dim zone. Rays beyond the critical angle cannot escape and are totally reflected, producing "a sharp ring of halation." Plate photographers saw lamps as "bright spots surrounded by a dark ring beyond which is another bright ring" ([Mees, Fundamentals of Photography, Ch. XI](https://chestofbooks.com/arts/photography/The-Fundamentals-Of-Photography/Chapter-XI-Halation.html)). A 1930s trade source states the scaling plainly: "the thickness of the support will determine the size of the ring." When thin nitrocellulose film replaced glass, "the halation rings were so reduced in size that they appeared more as a spreading of the blur" ([International Projectionist, p.63–64](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/internationalpro12345finn_0064)).

Standard optics turns this into numbers. For a base of thickness t and index n, the critical angle is θc = arcsin(1/n), and a ray at internal angle θ returns at radius r = 2t·tan θ. Below θc only Fresnel reflection returns light, about **4% at normal incidence** for n ≈ 1.5. At and beyond θc reflection is 100%, so the profile steps up sharply at r_c = 2t·tan θc. For triacetate (n ≈ 1.48) that is **r_c ≈ 1.83·t**, and for PET (n ≈ 1.65) about 1.52·t. The refractive indices are common literature values that the notes could not source. Base thicknesses are documented: Kodak lists 135 Ektar and Portra 400 at **0.13 mm acetate**, 120 at 0.10–0.11 mm, and sheet ESTAR at 0.19 mm ([Ektar E-4046](https://business.kodakmoments.com/sites/default/files/files/products/e4046_ektar_100.pdf); [Portra 400 E-4050](https://business.kodakmoments.com/sites/default/files/files/products/e4050_portra_400.pdf)). Ilford HP5 Plus is 125 µm in 35 mm, 110 µm in 120 and 180 µm polyester in sheet ([Ilford HP5 Plus](https://www.ilfordphoto.com/amfile/file/download/file/1903/product/691/)). On 135, the first TIR ring therefore falls at roughly **230–260 µm**, about 0.7% of a 36 mm frame width. Kodak gives no thickness for Vision3, only "acetate safety base with rem-jet backing" ([Kodak 7219 datasheet](https://www.kodak.com/content/products-brochures/Film/VISION3-500T-Color-Negative-Film-7219-TECHNICAL-DATA.pdf)).

Beyond the ring the signal falls off. Scattered light is forward-peaked, so few rays travel at grazing angles, and those that do take longer, more absorbing paths. Repeated TIR bounces add fainter rings at multiples of r_c. A backing removes the sharp ring and leaves a diffuse residual: on a backed plate Mees still saw "irregular blotches of light reflected from the back of the glass." Two caveats apply to modern film. First, no measured profile of the TIR ring on modern colour negative was found, and on thin film the ring is softened by re-entry scatter, emulsion thickness and finite source size, which is why observers mostly describe a smooth glow ([HolyGrain](https://www.holygrain.com/blog/what-is-film-halation-in-cinema/)). Second, sources disagree on emphasis. Analog.cafe treats base–air TIR as the main reflector, whereas Dehancer stresses camera interiors and pressure plates ([Analog.cafe](https://www.analog.cafe/r/halation-in-film-photography-i3q4); [Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/)). Both mechanisms plausibly contribute. TIR produces the ring, and a chrome pressure plate adds near-normal reflection of the sub-critical cone that escapes the base ([Wikipedia, Anti-halation backing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-halation_backing)).

Halation must be kept separate from three look-alikes. Irradiation (turbidity) is grain scatter inside the emulsion: a short core of tens of µm that occurs with or without a base. Mees treats it as distinct ([Mees](https://chestofbooks.com/arts/photography/The-Fundamentals-Of-Photography/Chapter-XI-Halation.html)), and Benke & McKellar model it with small-angle radiative transfer using a Gaussian forward lobe ([Applied Optics 1990](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-29-1-151)). Lens flare, veiling glare and diffusion filters such as Black Pro-Mist act on scene light before the film. They are roughly RGB-neutral and "cannot" produce the red fringe ([Prodigium](https://www.prodigium-pictures.com/blog/insight09-halation-on-film-digitally-imitating-it)). For the editor, this means three separate kernels, applied in order: lens/diffusion on scene light, irradiation per layer, then halation.

## Layer order and anti-halation layers decide colour and strength

Colour negative is coated, from the lens down, as blue-sensitive, yellow filter, green-sensitive, interlayer, red-sensitive, substratum, base, and a back anti-halation layer ([NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/node/4236)). Kodak's patent describes "the red layer adjacent to the antihalation layer" ([US 5246823](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5246823)). Light reaching the base has already lost its blue to the yellow filter and been depleted by absorption in the upper layers. On the way back it meets the red layer first. This produces **R ≫ G ≫ B** naturally, without any tint parameter. The extra red exposure forms cyan dye in the negative, which prints or scans as a red fringe. Dehancer reports the halo is orange near the overexposed core, where green is also exposed, and redder further out ([Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/)). Some vendor explanations get the cause wrong. Color Finale claims "red light scatters the most" ([Color Finale](https://colorfinale.com/blog/post/cf-halation-11-24)), which contradicts scattering physics, since shorter wavelengths scatter more. The real cause is penetration depth and layer order, as HolyGrain states ([HolyGrain](https://www.holygrain.com/blog/what-is-film-halation-in-cinema/)). Source spectrum matters for the same reason: Dehancer reports obvious halos around incandescent lamps and weak ones around LEDs, which emit little deep red. That observation is plausible but unmeasured, and it favours a spectral or at least per-channel model over a fixed red tint.

Manufacturers suppress halation in three ways ([US 5246823](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5246823); [US 3392021A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3392021A/en)):

- **Rem-jet**, a removable backing of carbon black in an alkali-soluble cellulose-acetate half-ester binder, which absorbs at the support–air interface. Motion picture stocks use it ([Bard & Dunn](https://journal.smpte.org/periodicals/Journal%20of%20the%20SMPTE/80/7/11/)).
- **A dyed or grey base.** Kodak says its density ranges from "barely detectable" to about 0.2 OD ([Kodak Film Essentials](https://125px.com/docs/motionpicture/kodak_guides/US_plugins_acrobat_en_motion_newsletters_filmEss_04_How-film-makes-image.pdf)).
- **An antihalation undercoat (AHU)** of grey or blue colloidal silver, or dyes, between base and emulsion. The patent's example blue-silver AHU at 25 mg/ft² has densities of **1.62 B, 2.01 G and 2.44 R**, chosen to absorb red most strongly for the adjoining red layer.

A useful quantitative point comes from the grey-base era. A base absorbing "approximately 37 per cent" cut halation light to "under 23 per cent", because the light crosses the base twice ([International Projectionist](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/internationalpro12345finn_0064)). Halation gain therefore scales as roughly 10^(−2D) for an absorbing base of density D, and 10^(−D) for an absorber that acts only once before reflection. This gives a principled way to turn an "anti-halation strength" control into a physical density.

Remove the backing and the whole mechanism comes back. CineStill 800T is Vision3 500T 5219 with the rem-jet removed so it can be processed in C-41. It shows "red halos around point light sources" and even an orange cast on "bright, overcast skies" ([Analog.Cafe 800T review](https://analog.cafe/r/cinestill-800t-film-review-dske); [Phillip Reeve](https://phillipreeve.net/blog/analogue-adventures-part-7-cinestill-800t/)). Silbersalz35 500T, whose rem-jet stays on during exposure and comes off in processing, showed no halos in the same reviewer's comparison. Even with a backing, halation does not vanish, because carbon "is not perfectly black" and leaves a shorter-range residual ([Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-halation_backing)). No document gives the rem-jet optical density or the residual halation fraction for Vision3.

## Exposure dependence is emergent: a few percent becomes visible 4–7 stops up

Because halation is optical redistribution, halation exposure is proportional to source exposure: H = k·(E ⊛ PSF). It should be added in linear exposure before log E and the H&D curve. Claims that halation is leftover light after grains "max out" their absorption ([Prodigium](https://www.prodigium-pictures.com/blog/insight09-halation-on-film-digitally-imitating-it)) conflict with this picture. The fraction of light transmitted is set by layer optical density, not by exposure level. Qualitative reports fit the linear model. Halation grows with source brightness and scene contrast and shows most against dark surrounds ([Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/)). It is "most evident when light sources are in focus" ([CineStill](https://help.cinestillfilm.com/hc/en-us/articles/360029185291-What-is-Halation)). It looks larger on 16 mm than on 35 mm or 65 mm because its size is fixed in µm on the film.

The visibility threshold follows from the numbers. With k ≈ 1–8%, the halo matches the surrounding exposure only where the source is roughly **4–7 stops** brighter than its surround. That is why it appears around practicals and speculars, not ordinary whites. This is an inference. No source states a measured threshold in stops for any stock. Vision3 has enough highlight latitude to keep sources on the curve: Kodak quotes at least 3½ stops above a white card that is itself 2⅓ stops over normal, about **+5.8 stops over grey** ([Vision3 5219 brochure](https://www.kodak.com/content/products-brochures/motion-picture/KODAK-VISION3-500T-5219-7219-brochure.pdf)). In practice the core of a lamp sits on the shoulder and looks white while its halo lands on the toe of the dark surround.

Clipping causes a related problem. If the input raw is clipped, the source's true energy is lost and halation is under-driven. spektrafilm adds a highlight-boost stage before scatter to "reconstruct pre-clip irradiance", using y = x + max·k·(exp(a·dx) − a·dx − 1) above a knee ([spektrafilm](https://github.com/andreavolpato/spektrafilm)). A scene-linear float editor should include something equivalent, ideally tied to its highlight-reconstruction module.

## spektrafilm is the reference design, and it is GPL-3.0

Most commercial tools publish control lists but not algorithms. Dehancer exposes Source Limiter, Background Gain, Local and Global Diffusion, Hue and Defringe ([Dehancer](https://blog.dehancer.com/articles/halation/)). FilmConvert Nitrate offers sensitivity, strength, hue and softness ([Newsshooter](https://www.newsshooter.com/2023/11/06/filmconvert-nitrate-halation/)). FilmBox has Gauge, Radius, Strength and an "Aura" for unbacked stocks ([Video Village](https://videovillage.com/learn/filmbox/full-guide/negative/halation)). Threshold or key controls are the norm, and the 2026 Deep Analog paper's soft-threshold, per-channel-blur halation, which its authors admit is "not a fitted optical point-spread model," was named by expert reviewers as a weak point ([arXiv 2608.14702](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14702v1)). Blender's GPU Bloom is threshold-driven and explicitly not energy-conserving: a highlight of 10 produces about 35 ([Blender PR #119128](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/pulls/119128)).

spektrafilm, by Andrea Volpato, is the exception. Its LICENSE is GPLv3, while its JSON profiles are CC BY-SA 4.0 and its LUTs carry a separate non-resale licence. The pipeline in `filming.py` runs as follows: spectral film raw exposure, exposure compensation, highlight boost, camera diffusion filter, lens blur, **halation**, then `log10`, development, and grain in density space ([spektrafilm](https://github.com/andreavolpato/spektrafilm); [whitepaper](https://spektrafilm.114c.de/technical/)). Its two optical stages are:

| Stage | Formula | Default parameters |
|---|---|---|
| Scatter (irradiation), energy-preserving | E₁ = (1−s)E₀ + s[(1−w)·G(σ_c)⊛E₀ + w·Exp(λ)⊛E₀] | σ_c = 2.2/2.0/1.6 µm RGB; λ = 9.3/9.7/9.1 µm; w = 0.78/0.65/0.67 |
| Halation, multi-bounce | E₂ = E₁ + A·Σₖ aₖ·G(σ_h√k)⊛E₁, aₖ ∝ ρ^(k−1), optionally ÷(1+a_tot) | A = 0.05/0.015/0 RGB; σ_h = 65 µm; N = 3; ρ = 0.5; renormalize on |

The presets tie strength to the type of anti-halation layer. Values for a₁ in red are about 0.005–0.02 for strong backing (Vision3, modern colour negative), 0.02–0.08 for weak backing, and 0.08–0.25 with none (rem-jet removed). The preset triples are (0.015, 0.005, 0), (0.08, 0.02, 0) and (0.30, 0.10, 0.015). σ_h is about 65 µm for still triacetate and about 50 µm for cine. Radii are specified in µm and converted through `pixel_size_um`, so the result is independent of resolution. The exponential tail is approximated by three Gaussians: (0.1633, 0.536λ), (0.6496, 1.5236λ) and (0.1870, 2.7684λ). The code calls these "placeholder fits." Large Gaussians use a Young–van Vliet recursive IIR filter, O(1) per pixel with about 1e-3 error.

Three things in spektrafilm need caution. First, its priors cite "private halation notes," so the numbers are informed tuning, not published measurement. Second, its σ_h of 50–65 µm is much smaller than the 230–260 µm TIR ring radius derived above. The author's earlier forum example used σ = 200 µm, with 3%/0.3%/0.1% RGB and 8% red for CineStill ([pixls.us](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/spectral-film-simulations-from-scratch/48209)), and the vkdt port deliberately enlarged the radius to match a reference photo ([pixls.us p.17](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/spectral-film-simulations-from-scratch/48209?page=17)). The halo size is therefore genuinely unsettled. Third, the presets describe cine stock as PET, while Kodak lists Vision3 as acetate. The Kodak datasheet should take precedence over the preset comment.

The hotgluebanjo halation-dctl README sets out a design philosophy close to spektrafilm's. It requires scene-linear input, uses "Exponential falloff – Not just a Gaussian blur," avoids edge detection, and lists common failures: "bad falloff," two-colour "banding" in the fading halo, "excessive highlight tinting" and "overzealous edge detection or keying" ([halation-dctl](https://github.com/hotgluebanjo/halation-dctl)). **The repository has no licence, so its code must not be copied**, but its list of failures works well as a test checklist.

## A recommended design for the Rust/wgpu pipeline

The evidence supports a model with two tiers, both in f32 scene-linear exposure per film layer, placed after lens and diffusion effects and before the log/H&D stage, with grain after development. The default tier follows spektrafilm: energy-preserving scatter, then additive multi-bounce Gaussian halation with optional renormalization, and per-channel strengths chosen by an anti-halation preset (strong, weak or none). An optional physical tier can replace the halation Gaussians with a geometric kernel parameterised by base thickness t, index n and backing density D. That kernel has a weak Fresnel inner disc for r < 2t·tan θc, a TIR ring at r_c weighted by a forward-peaked angular distribution with the 2t·sec²θ Jacobian, and decaying repeat rings at k·r_c. The kernel is then convolved with the scatter core to soften the ring, and its gain is scaled by 10^(−D) or 10^(−2D) depending on where the absorber sits. Both tiers follow the same physical constraints: radii come from µm and the frame size, colour comes from layer order rather than a hue slider, and there is no threshold. Smaller formats then get larger relative halos automatically.

On wgpu, the IIR filter that spektrafilm uses is serial per row and awkward on a GPU. A mip pyramid with per-level Gaussian weights, in the style of dual-filter bloom, approximates a sum of Gaussians whose σ doubles at each level. That fits the exponential and multi-bounce mixtures cheaply. Separable shared-memory FIR is practical up to about 30–60 px, and FFT is the accurate option for very large kernels. Blender's realtime compositor took the pyramid route and saw FFT as the future accurate path ([Blender PR #106042](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/pulls/106042)). Upsampling with tent or 13-tap filters avoids blocky halos, though that guidance comes from general graphics practice rather than halation sources. Running chromatic-aberration correction before halation prevents purple fringes from being amplified into coloured halos, which is the purpose of Dehancer's "Defringe" step.

## Validation needs self-made bracketed point-source scans

No standard measures halation. ISO 6328 covers tribar resolving power, not MTF or halation ([ISO 6328:2000](https://iteh.es/catalog/standards/iso/adb0cb60-470f-477e-985e-f1294777c17c/iso-6328-2000)). No public dataset pairs known scene radiance with linear scans for halation. HAL-Net uses 100 hand-picked CineStill 800T scans from Flickr with pseudo-labels, and its radius prediction did worse than baseline (MAE 0.015 vs 0.012) ([HAL-Net](https://ahadstfu-hal-net.hf.space/)). BlueNeg offers 16-bit DNG 35 mm negatives under a CC BY-style licence, but it does not identify stocks or target halation ([BlueNeg](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/supplemental/Liu_BlueNeg_A_35mm_ICCV_2025_supplemental.pdf)). Web scans are useful only for qualitative checks. One such check comes from a pixls.us user: real 800T "keeps clarity in its midtones and shadows," whereas the vkdt emulation washed them out ([pixls.us](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/spectral-film-simulations-from-scratch/48209?page=17)).

A credible calibration protocol, assembled from the notes, has five steps:

1. **Photograph a small halogen-backlit pinhole in a dark room.** Halogen is used for its red content. Bracket from +0 to +10 stops, and shoot the same lens and focus on a digital body as a control for lens flare.
2. **Add a backlit knife-edge.** It gives an edge-spread function, which differentiates to a line spread function. The tail beyond about 100 µm is halation, while the core under 20–30 µm is turbidity plus the lens.
3. **Include an in-frame calibration.** US 4397554 uses concentric ND rings of 1.13, 1.70 and 2.36 OD ([US 4397554](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4397554)).
4. **Scan linearly and convert to exposure.** Use no sharpening or ICE, and convert density back to relative exposure through the stock's curve. Then deconvolve the digital control.
5. **Shoot matched pairs.** Shoot Vision3 with rem-jet and CineStill 800T to isolate the base-reflection component. Shoot 135 (130 µm) and 120 (100–110 µm) of the same Kodak stock to test whether the ring radius scales with t.

An indirect cross-check is also possible. A halo of a few hundred µm should show as a low-frequency shelf at about 2–5 cycles/mm in the red MTF curve on Kodak datasheets, and digitising those charts would test this. Nobody has done it in the sources found.

## Conclusion

The physics is settled and simple: halation is linear, red-first, sized by the base, and suppressed by an absorber whose effect can be modelled as a density. The practical uncertainty is almost entirely empirical, in particular the size of the halo. Geometry predicts a TIR ring near 240 µm on 135 film, the best open emulator defaults to a 65 µm Gaussian, and a later port enlarged it by eye. Neither figure has been checked against a measured modern-stock profile, because none has been published. An editor that exposes t, n and backing density as its parameters, rather than "radius" and "hue," can both reproduce existing looks and become the first tool whose halation is fitted to real measurements.

The validation plan is therefore not an optional extra. It is the main way this project can improve on existing tools. A small set of bracketed pinhole and edge scans across 135 and 120, with and without rem-jet, would test the √k multi-bounce model against the TIR-ring model and decide whether the ring is visible at all on thin film. It would also produce the first public per-stock halation fractions, which every current tool, open or commercial, is guessing at.
